## 1. What the paper is for
Mixed-variable symplectic (MVS) integrators (Wisdom and Holman 1991, "WH91") need a constant step for all bodies, so they cannot resolve close encounters; changing the step during an encounter destroys the surrogate Hamiltonian and the time reversibility, and a step varied with phase-space state destroys symplecticity (p.2067-2068). Levison and Duncan's earlier RMVS gave up reversibility and the surrogate Hamiltonian to handle test-particle encounters (p.2069). SyMBA (symplectic massive body algorithm) keeps all three properties (symplectic, time reversible, a surrogate Hamiltonian H + H_err), keeps MVS speed when bodies are apart, and subdivides the step recursively for each close pair, for massive bodies (abstract, p.2067; sections 5 and 7).

## 2. Method, with equations
### 2.1 Background (section 2, pp.2067-2069)
- Operator composition (3): exp(tau {., H0 + H1}) ~ exp((tau/2){., H0}) exp(tau {., H1}) exp((tau/2){., H0}). Surrogate Hamiltonian H~ = H + H_err; for the second-order scheme H_err = (tau^2/12) { {H0, H1}, H0 + H1/2 } + O(tau^4) (4). The series generally does not converge but is well represented by its leading terms for small tau.
- Notation (7a): E_AB(tau) = E_A(tau/2) E_B(tau) E_A(tau/2) (E_i(tau) = evolution under H_i for time tau); (7b) the same with the roles swapped.
- T+V split H = H_T(p) + H_V(q) gives leapfrog (10); for solar-system accuracy of about 1e-5 in energy it needs a step of about 1e-3 of the smallest orbital period (p.2068).
- MVS split H = H_Kep + H_int (11)-(13), Jacobi coordinates (primed). Speed comes from |H_int| << |H_Kep| by the mass ratio, which allows a step larger by a factor mu^(-1/2) than leapfrog, typically about 1/20 of the shortest orbital period (p.2069). It loses its advantage when (i) a body other than body 1 comes close to the Sun (the first term of H_int is zero only for body 1) or (ii) two bodies approach (the second term).

### 2.2 Multiple-time-step splitting of the force (section 3, pp.2069-2071)
Choose cutoff radii R_1 > R_2 > ... and decompose H_V = sum_k V_k, equivalently the force F = sum_k F_k with:
1. F(r) = sum_{k=0}^infinity F_k(r) for all r;
2. F_k (k > 0) is zero for r > R_k;
3. F_k is "softer" than F_(k+1) (Fig. 1).
Each F_k is applied with its own step tau_k, with tau_k/tau_(k+1) an integer; the implementation uses a constant integer M (p.2069).
Recursive second-order scheme (16): E_(Sigma_0)(tau_0) ~ E_(V_0)(tau_0/2) E_(Sigma_1)(tau_0) E_(V_0)(tau_0/2), where E_(Sigma_i) is evolution under H_T + sum_(k >= i) V_k, itself approximated by M steps of length tau_i of the same form, with E_(V_i)(tau_i) as the outer half-steps; E_(Sigma_(n+1)) reduces to the plain drift when r > R_(n+1). If r > R_1 throughout the step this is ordinary leapfrog. The step "appears" to vary but the high-frequency terms are always present with identity action when F_k = 0, so the method is a fixed-step symplectic scheme with all the properties of p.2068.
Error Hamiltonian (17): H_err = sum_(k=0)^infinity (tau_k^2/12) { {V_k, H_T}, H_T + (1/2) V_k + sum_(l=k+1)^infinity V_l } + O(tau_k^4). Conditions deduced from it (p.2070): (a) V_k needs smooth second derivatives (F_k smooth first derivatives), smooth fourth derivatives if the O(tau^4) terms are to be well defined; (b) if |F_k| ~ r^alpha for r << R_k, then alpha > 2 so the error does not grow rapidly as r falls. Both were confirmed experimentally as required.

### 2.3 The two force decompositions (eqs. 18 to 23, p.2070)
Write H_V = V~_0 + (V~_1 - V~_0) + (V~_2 - V~_1) + ... (18), V_0 = V~_0, V_k = V~_k - V~_(k-1); F~_k = -dV~_k/dq (the force in the shell below R_k is F~_(k-1)... indices as printed).
Approach 1 (improved Skeel and Biesiadecki 1994): V~_(k-1) = -1/r for r >= R_k, matched to a truncated Taylor expansion of -1/r below R_k, with s = (r/R_k)^n (19). Printed forms, with q the separation vector:
- l = 2, n = 4 (21): F~_(k-1) = -q/r^3 for r >= R_k; -[9 - 5 (r/R_k)^4] (r/R_k)^2 q / (4 R_k^3) for r < R_k. Satisfies the minimal conditions; evaluates with powers of r^2 only.
- l = n = 4, smoother (22): F~_(k-1) = -[663 - 1105 (r/R_k)^4 + 765 (r/R_k)^8 - 195 (r/R_k)^12] (r/R_k)^2 q / (128 R_k^3) for r < R_k. The authors say it sometimes behaves slightly better but is less tested, so they do not recommend it over (23).
- The Skeel-Biesiadecki forms (l = 1, n = 1 or 2) do not satisfy the minimal conditions.
Approach 2 (partition function, adopted for the rest of the paper, l = 1) (23): F~_(k-1) = -q/r^3 for r >= R_k; -f_l((R_k - r)/(R_k - R_(k+1))) q/r^3 for R_(k+1) <= r < R_k; 0 for r < R_(k+1). The partition function f_l is the lowest-order polynomial with f_l(0) = 1, f_l(1) = 0 and zero derivatives up to the l-th at 0 and 1: f_1(x) = 2x^3 - 3x^2 + 1, f_3(x) = 20x^7 - 70x^6 + 84x^5 - 35x^4 + 1. It is derivable from a potential when f depends on r only. COMPUTED: f_1(0.5) = 0.5, f_1(x) + f_1(1-x) = 1, f_1'(0) = f_1'(1) = 0, f_3(0.5) = 0.5 (20/128 - 70/64 + 84/32 - 35/16 + 1 = 0.15625 - 1.09375 + 2.625 - 2.1875 + 1 = 0.5), f_3(1) = 20 - 70 + 84 - 35 + 1 = 0.
For N bodies (24)-(25) each pair term is split independently: H_V = sum_(i<j) sum_k V_ijk, so only the pair in an encounter is sampled at the shorter step.

### 2.4 Why the democratic-heliocentric (DH) variant is needed (sections 3 and 4, pp.2071-2073)
Applying the multiple-step scheme to the WH91 split fails (p.2071): in Jacobi variables each body revolves about a different centre with a different effective central mass, which the first term of H_int (eq. 13) compensates; that term is applied at the low frequency while the pair terms are applied at the high frequency in an encounter, so the compensation breaks. The fix is a split in which all bodies orbit the same centre with the same central mass.
Canonical transformation (27)-(31): new positions Q_i = q_i - q_0 (heliocentric) for i > 0 and Q_0 = centre of mass; new momenta P_i = barycentric momenta for i > 0 and P_0 = total momentum. Then H = H_Kep + H_Sun + H_int:
- (32a) H_Kep = sum_(i=1)^n ( |P_i|^2/(2 m_i) - G m_i m_0/|Q_i| ),
- (32b) H_Sun = (1/(2 m_0)) | sum_(i=1)^n P_i |^2,
- (32c) H_int = - sum_(i=1)^(n-1) sum_(j=i+1)^n G m_i m_j / |Q_i - Q_j|.
Second-order single-step scheme (33): E_Sun(tau/2) E_int(tau/2) E_Kep(tau) E_int(tau/2) E_Sun(tau/2) (the order of Sun and int does not matter since {H_Sun, H_int} = 0). Step: (1) linear drift of each body by (tau/(2 m_0)) sum P_i; (2) kick by the other bodies (not the Sun) for tau/2; (3) Kepler advance for tau; (4) kick for tau/2; (5) drift. Because the positions are heliocentric and the central mass is the same for all bodies this is the democratic heliocentric method (Chambers 1999 uses the same coordinates and cites this paper for the name). Touma and Wisdom 1994 used the same Q_i, P_i but moved the |P_i|^2/(2 m_i) terms, giving different central masses (p.2072 footnote).
Stated advantages: the ordering of bodies does not matter (the MVS energy conservation degrades if bodies are not ordered by increasing semimajor axis, Chambers and Wetherill 1998); with crossing orbits the MVS ordering cannot be updated without changing the surrogate Hamiltonian, while DH has no such problem (p.2072). Stated disadvantage: DH does not handle small pericentric distances well because even for the Kepler problem H_Sun is non-zero and the error grows as |P_i| grows near the Sun (p.2072). MVS is exact for the Kepler problem and handles small pericentre only for massless particles or the innermost planet (p.2073).

### 2.5 SyMBA (section 5, pp.2073-2074)
SyMBA = DH Hamiltonian with H_int replaced by the V_ijk decomposition (34): V_k = sum_(i<j) V_ijk. Time reversible, symplectic, with surrogate Hamiltonian H~ = H + H_err (35):
H_err = (tau_0^2/12) { {H_Sun, H_Kep}, H_Kep + (1/2) H_Sun } + sum_(k=0)^infinity (tau_k^2/12) { {V_k, H_Kep}, H_Kep + (1/2) V_k + sum_(l=k+1)^infinity V_l } + O(tau_k^4);
the first term belongs to H_Sun, the second to the pair interactions. Consequences listed (p.2073-2074):
1. V_ijk must satisfy the conditions of section 2.2.
2. For the Kepler problem the energy error grows as the pericentre falls (Fig. 3 top), so the overall step tau_0 must resolve the pericentre passage of every body for all time; a limitation the authors "have thus far been unsuccessful" in removing.
3. For a very close pair the relative speed scales as r_ij^(-1/2), giving a dominant error term scaling as tau_k^2 / r_ij^4 near r_ij ~ R_k; choosing R_k/R_(k+1) = (tau_k/tau_(k+1))^(1/2) makes the energy error independent of separation.
Adopted parameters (p.2074): M = tau_k/tau_(k+1) = 3; R_k/R_(k+1) = 2.08, equivalent to the step decreasing as r_ij^(3/2) (the steeper choice in item 3 is "computationally very expensive" and needed only for extreme encounters); R_1 about 3 mutual Hill radii. COMPUTED: 3^(2/3) = 2.080, which is the ratio giving tau proportional to r^(3/2); the item-3 choice would be 3^(1/2) = 1.73, and Fig. 1's illustrative ratio is 2^(1/2).
Limitation to carry forward: the paper's own tests omit encounters closer than the DH Kepler limit, "as long as the pericentric distances did not get very small" (p.2077), and the lunar-accretion test removes nine objects with initial pericentre inside the Earth's radius (p.2076).

## 5. Does it supply Chambers's T2? No
Chambers's T2 (the scaled outer Solar System, masses x50, step 11.05 d, r_crit = 3 R_H) is described as a repeat of this paper's test (Chambers p.797). Checking this paper (section 6.2, p.2074): the printed inputs are only (a) the Sun and four giant planets, (b) masses increased by a factor of 50, (c) "initially on nearly circular orbits", (d) step 0.03 yr, (e) a stated outcome sequence. The planets' semimajor axes, eccentricities, angles, epoch and which values were used for "nearly circular" are NOT printed in either paper, and no table is given. A reproduction needs the planets' initial elements and masses, which the authors presumably took from a standard Solar System set (INFERRED, not stated). So T2 is not reproducible from the two papers as printed. What can be done:
- Weak qualitative reproduction: start from the real giant-planet states at a documented epoch with masses x50, step 0.03 yr (Chambers's 11.05 d is 0.0303 yr, COMPUTED 11.05/365.25), and check the printed outcomes: Neptune-Saturn crossing at about 30 yr in this paper, energy error oscillating about zero with no secular growth, ejections within a few thousand years. The two papers' outcomes already differ: here Saturn leaves at 1920 yr and Uranus about 60 yr later; Chambers reports Neptune removed at about 1000 yr, then Uranus and Saturn, and states that the evolution is highly chaotic and changes with the step size (p.797). So a match of ejection order or time is not a pass criterion, only the absence of a secular energy trend and its order of magnitude are testable.
- The magnitude comparison: this paper's energy-error range (plot read, up to about 7e-6 with spikes) against Chambers's about +/-1.5e-6 (plot read, Chambers Fig. 2) are of the same order, consistent with Chambers's statement that his is "similar to SyMBA", but both are plot reads.
- D7 and Chambers's T3 are a closer pair than D6 and T2 because both are fully specified: Neptune circular at 30 au, particles at a = 36 au (Chambers, e = 0.18, perihelion about 29.5 au) or 36 to 40 au at perihelion 30 au (this paper); Jacobi constant error 3e-6 over 1e6 yr (Chambers, step 5 yr) against 1 part in 29 000 over up to 1e9 yr with median lifetime 2.9e6 yr (this paper, step 2 yr). These bound a Jacobi-constant conservation control from two sources.

## 6. Reconciliation against the project's integrators
Repeats the findings of the Chambers digest (checked by grep on 2026-10-04 and not changed by this paper): REBOUND is used with IAS15 only (`nbody/propagator.py`, `nbody/jovian.py`, `data/validation/v3_3d*.py`, verify scripts); scipy DOP853, LSODA and a compiled DOP853 with a transition matrix (`search/titania_oberon_realeph_895.py`) elsewhere; no MERCURIUS, WHFast or other symplectic integrator. In every lane the spacecraft is massless and the moons and planets are prescribed, so the massive-body encounter problem of SyMBA does not arise. The project does not use any symplectic integrator (I did not check which integrators REBOUND's current release offers; that is not in this paper).
Additional points specific to this paper:
- The test-particle part of the paper (D7) is the closest to project usage: massless particles encountering one massive body, tracked by the Jacobi constant. The project's CR3BP/BCR4BP cores have the same invariant in the autonomous case.
- The paper's central caution, that a fixed outer step must resolve the pericentre of every body about the central mass, applies to any project lane that uses a fixed or coarsely tabulated step: the `#895` ephemeris table (cubic Hermite) and fixed-node multiple shooting. Not to adaptive IAS15 or DOP853.
- The DH method's weakness near the central mass (error rises as the pericentre falls, D3 and D4) is a statement about a split integrator and has no counterpart in the project's lanes; it argues against using a SyMBA-type integrator for the Uranian planet-centred arcs that include near-planet passes (INFERRED).

## 7. Techniques applicable to the project's problems
Read from `data/OUTSTANDING.md` before mapping: `#900`, `#908`, `#924`, `#928`, `#929`, plus `#895` and `#890`.
Summary: SyMBA is not a candidate integrator for any current project computation (massless particle, prescribed bodies, no encounter between massive bodies). It supplies (a) a design for a smooth, tested pair-force switching function with published conditions, (b) published energy and Jacobi-constant control cases for a close-encounter integrator, (c) a rule for choosing the encounter shell radii from the step, and (d) an explicit statement of which conditions on the force split a multi-rate scheme needs.

### 7.1 #928 (regularised propagator and transition matrix for close passes)
- The paper's treatment of a close pass is time-step subdivision by shells, not regularisation; it does not give a transition matrix. For `#928` the useful content is the error scaling: near R_k the dominant error term goes as tau_k^2 / r^4 with relative speed about r^(-1/2) (section 5, item 3), so a constant-step method needs tau proportional to r^2 to keep the energy error constant, while the adopted shells give tau proportional to r^(3/2). This is the Sundman-versus-plain comparison in another form: a constant-error step law for the Kepler pass is tau ~ r^2; a Sundman transformation with dt = r^p ds gives step proportional to r^p. `#928`'s planned comparison of plain, Sundman and Levi-Civita on the `#896` orbit 12i can use these two exponents (3/2 and 2) as a labelled reference for the step-size law each method implies. Not a golden.
- Test for `#928` taken from the paper: the DH Kepler-error curve (D3) is a case where the integrator error is monotone in pericentre. The analogue for a regularised propagator is that its error is flat in pericentre; fitting the error against pericentre distance over several decades for plain, Sundman and Levi-Civita propagation is a falsifiable control that the regularised version is regular. The paper's own numbers (D3) are not a target for it.

### 7.2 #929 (integrator controls through one moon flyby)
Fits the controls listed there and adds published evidence:
- (a) Reversibility through a flyby: SyMBA's selling point is exact time reversibility through encounters (p.2072). The project's adaptive integrators are not time reversible, so a forward-and-back return error is a direct measure of how far they are from that ideal; use it as the metric, not as a pass/fail against SyMBA.
- (b) Three-way comparison: the paper's method of checking against "several other well-established codes" (p.2074, section 6) is the same pattern; a statistical agreement criterion is the paper's own standard for chaotic cases, a pointwise one is stronger and available for a single flyby.
- (c) Table and tolerance sensitivity in the `#900` rebuild: the paper's condition that shell radii R_k and step tau_k be tied (R_k/R_(k+1) = (tau_k/tau_(k+1))^(1/2) or the softer 2.08 choice) is the equivalent rule for a table: the table step times the moon's speed must be small compared with the length scale at which the force changes, which near a flyby is the periapsis distance.
- Published control values for a close-encounter integrator that `#929` can cite (all from this paper): Jacobi constant conserved to one part in 29 000 worst case over 1e9 yr with a 2 yr step (D7); energy error below 1e-6 for a 3200-orbit tight binary with a 0.01 yr step (D5). These are for a different integrator and problem class; use them only to see that an error level of this order is ordinary in the literature, not to set a pass level.

### 7.3 #900 (rebuild the V4 moon-tour validation lane)
- The paper reinforces the `#900` design rule: the pair force is never softened or zeroed inside the encounter radius; it is applied in full on a finer step. The V4 lanes' practice of zeroing a moon's gravity inside its Hill sphere is the opposite of this and the paper's conditions (force smoothness, |F_k| decreasing faster than r^2 at small r, section 2.2) do not licence it.
- Bookkeeping point repeated from the Chambers digest: the DH split attributes the central body's motion to H_Sun = |sum P_i|^2/(2 m_0), counted once; the double-count of moon mass in `#894` is the planet-centred counterpart. A test: with the lane's GM set, the barycentric momentum sum used for the planet's reflex must equal the sum implied by the same masses (a Newton's-third-law identity).
- Cutoff rule check for ephemeris tables: R_1 about 3 mutual Hill radii (p.2074) is a sanity bound for the radius inside which the table spacing must be fine; for Uranian moons the Hill radii are of order 5 000 to 15 000 km (the `v4_uranus.py` docstring), so a table step times a moon's speed of order 1 km/s resolving a 3 R_H region needs a step of hours, not days. INFERRED; compute for the actual moon before use.

### 7.4 #908 (capture-sweep sampling)
- No direct transfer: the sweeps are many massless-particle propagations under a prescribed perturber and do not need a symplectic integrator. The relevant lesson is D7: a Jacobi-constant check per particle over the whole run, with a stated worst-case figure, is the paper's standard of "well conserved"; a sweep that records the worst Jacobi-constant variation per initial condition (in the CR3BP cases) gets a free integrator health flag for each point and would catch the kind of isolated artefact in the project's memory on isolated flips in sweeps.
- If speed is ever the limit, the same caveat as in the Chambers digest applies: a Kepler-plus-kick split requires the perturbation to be small against the Kepler term. SyMBA's evidence (D3, D4) shows the error grows with falling pericentre of any body about the central mass; capture orbits are by definition near-parabolic about the planet with low pericentre, which is the case the DH scheme handles worst, so it is a worse fit than the Chambers hybrid for `#908` (INFERRED).
