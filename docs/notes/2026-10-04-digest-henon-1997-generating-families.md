## 1. Definitions, exactly as the book sets them

### 1.1 Periodic orbits, families, period-in-family (READ pp.5-12)

- Restricted problem (section 2.2, p.5): masses M1 = 1 - mu, M2 = mu, M3 = 0; rotating axes (x, y) with the ORIGIN IN M1 (not in the centre of mass), M2 at (1, 0), angular velocity 1. Jacobi constant (eq. 2.4). Fixed axes (X, Y) coincide with (x, y) at t = 0 (eq. 2.5); the Moon is at (cos t, sin t) there.
- A "periodic solution" has R(T) = R(0) in the rotating axes. "A periodic orbit" is the set of solutions that differ by a time shift. Proposition 2.3.1: "Periodic orbits form one-parameter families."
- Definition 2.4.1: the period-in-family T* is "the limit of the minimal period T0 as one tends towards that orbit on the family". Definition 2.4.2: "A family of periodic orbits is defined by the two functions R(t, lambda) and T*(lambda)." The principle of positive definition (p.9): "a definition relating to orbits in a family should not be based on a negative property, such as an inequality". This governs every definition below.
- Principle of natural termination (section 2.5, p.10; Stromgren, Wintner, Birkhoff): a family is closed, or open with a natural termination where the dimension, the Jacobi constant or the period grows without limit, or has a reflection (it comes back over itself with a half-period shift). Proposition 2.5.1: "Starting from any given orbit in a family, one can move along the family in two and only two directions."
- Stability (section 2.8, p.14), READ and verbatim: with the surface of section y = 0 and the linearised return map of matrix (a b; c d), "a d - b c = 1" and "We call stability index the quantity z = (a + d)/2" (eq. 2.11). "The fixed point is stable if |z| < 1, unstable if |z| > 1." "We call critical orbit of the first kind an orbit for which z = +1, and critical orbit of the second kind an orbit for which z = -1." Proposition 2.8.1: "A critical orbit of the first kind corresponds either to an extremum in C or to an intersection with another family." Proposition 2.8.2: "A critical orbit of the second kind corresponds to a pseudo-intersection with another family with twice the period. It is also an extremum in C for this other family." So Hénon's z is half the trace (the same as the project's Barden-style nu if that is defined from the same full-period matrix; not checked) and equals half of Casoliva's k = lambda + 1/lambda.

### 1.2 Generating orbit, family of generating orbits, generating family (READ pp.14-16)

- Definition 2.9.1: "Q is a generating orbit if there exists a set of periodic orbits Q_mu, defined for 0 < mu <= mu0, such that Q_mu tends to Q for mu going to 0." A generating solution is the same with an origin of time.
- Hénon insists that generating orbits are not Keplerian orbits (p.15): "Generating orbits should not be confused with keplerian orbits: generating orbits correspond to mu going to 0, while keplerian orbits correspond to mu = 0; and these two cases are very different. This is a typical property of singular perturbation problems ... there exist keplerian orbits which are not generating orbits; generating orbits which are not keplerian orbits; and generating orbits which are also keplerian orbits."
- Proposition 2.9.1: "Generating orbits form one-parameter families." Two different groupings (p.15, verbatim): "Definition 2.9.2. Families of generating orbits are obtained from the periodic orbits for mu > 0 by first letting mu go to 0, thus obtaining generating orbits, and then grouping these generating orbits in families. Definition 2.9.3. Generating families are obtained by first grouping the periodic orbits for a given value mu > 0 into families, and then letting mu go to 0." The second kind is the one that predicts the families seen at positive mu (chapters 9 and 10); the first kind may end in other ways than natural termination or reflection.
- Species (section 2.10, pp.16-17), after the positive-definition rewrite: Definition 2.10.1 "A generating orbit belongs to the first species if it is a keplerian orbit." Definition 2.10.2 "A generating orbit belongs to the second species if at least one of its points coincides with M2." Definition 2.10.3 "A generating orbit belongs to the third species if it consists of the point M2 alone." The species are not exclusive (Fig. 2.4). The informal version (p.16): in the second species "some points of the orbit tend towards M2 while others remain at a finite distance. The generating orbit is then made of a succession of arcs, an arc being defined as a part of the generating orbit which begins and ends in M2. Each passage through M2 will be called a collision."
- Near a collision (p.18): "The generating orbit exhibits an angle ... at each collision: it is non-analytic. A neighbouring orbit for mu > 0 makes a sharp turn, approximately described by a hyperbolic motion around M2; but the motion is now analytic." And on the species at positive mu (p.18): "it is not possible to give a strict definition of species for mu > 0."
- The introduction's picture of the relation to small mu (pp.3-4, Fig. 1.1): near an ordinary generating orbit the family is "simply displaced by an amount of the order of mu"; near a bifurcation orbit "the four branches are joined in a new way, and the characteristics exhibit sharp, hyperbolic-like turns in the vicinity of Q ... the distance from Q is no longer simply of the order of mu". For j families through one orbit there are 2j branches, joined in pairs in (2j - 1)(2j - 3)...(3)(1) ways.

### 1.3 Arcs, deflection angle, types (READ pp.35-42)

- Definition 4.1.1: "An arc is a finite piece of keplerian orbit which begins and ends in a collision." Definition 4.1.2: "A basic arc is an arc which has no collisions except at its two ends." The Keplerian orbit carrying an arc is its supporting ellipse (carried by a fictitious body M4). The deflection angle (p.35): "Two consecutive arcs join at a collision; their tangents at the collision form an angle, generally different from zero. This angle, measured in rotating axes, will be called deflection angle." This is the project's demanded turn, measured in the rotating frame. When a deflection angle is zero the two basic arcs have the same supporting ellipse, "the velocity of M3 has the same direction before and after the collision; it has also the same modulus, because of the conservation of the Jacobi constant" (p.36). "(Note that there can be more than one collision per period.)" (p.35). A generating solution is a sequence of p arcs (eq. 4.1); an arc is defined by its supporting solution and the duration 2 tau_j = t_j'' - t_j' (eq. 4.2). Proposition 4.1.2: "A generating orbit is completely defined if we know the duration and either the initial or the final velocity of each arc."
- Hyperbolic and parabolic supporting orbits form one arc family, Sh (p.37), with C going from minus infinity to Cp = -0.720283 where it joins the elliptic family S00.
- Four types of elliptic supporting ellipse (section 4.2, p.38), by the number of common points with the unit circle (Fig. 4.1), r1 and r2 the pericentre and apocentre distance: type 1 if r1 < 1 < r2 (two intersection points); type 2 if tangent at one point; type 3 if the ellipse is the unit circle and the motion retrograde; type 4 if it is the unit circle and the motion direct. Velocity of M4 at the point P where it enters the unit circle: polar coordinates (V, gamma), gamma measured from the velocity of M2 (eq. 4.4, 4.5): 0 < gamma < pi, 0 < V < sqrt 2 (fixed axes, speed of M2 is 1). Jacobi constant (eqs. 4.6, 4.7): C = 2 + 2 V cos gamma - V^2 = 3 - (V cos gamma - 1)^2 - (V sin gamma)^2. Range (eq. 4.8): "-2 sqrt 2 < C < 3 for arcs of types 1 and 2, C = -1 for arcs of type 3, C = 3 for arcs of type 4." In rotating axes with polar coordinates (v, theta) of the velocity, theta measured from the y axis (section 8.2, p.139): V cos gamma = 1 + v cos theta, V sin gamma = v sin theta (eq. 8.2), and C = 3 - v^2 (eq. 8.3). So a generating orbit exists only for C less than 3, and the relative speed at every collision is sqrt(3 - C).
- Type 1 (section 4.3, p.41): P is "the point where M4 penetrates inside the unit circle"; Q is "the point where M4 emerges from the unit circle". Definition 4.3.2: "A S-arc begins and ends at two different points (in fixed axes); a T-arc begins and ends at the same point (in fixed axes)." Ingoing arcs begin at P, outgoing at Q: ingoing S-arc PQ, outgoing S-arc QP, ingoing T-arc PP, outgoing T-arc QQ. The book's own S and T labels are these (Definition 4.3.2); they are not the labels used in other papers' tables and must not be mapped by letter. An S-arc is symmetric: "A S-arc is made of two halves which are symmetrical of each other with respect to the x axis" (Proposition 4.3.1, p.42). A T-arc spans an integer I of the Moon's revolutions and an integer J of the particle's: "the duration of the arc is 2 pi I; the absolute mean motion of the supporting ellipse is J/I; and the semi-major axis is a = (I/J)^(2/3)" (eq. 4.49, p.55).
- Orbits and families of the second species (sections 4.7 and 4.8, pp.73-75): an orbit is a periodic sequence of arcs (eq. 4.81) "all arcs must have the same value of C"; "if we choose a value of C and a sequence of arcs having that value, we define an orbit"; it is simple if one minimal period holds one arc, composite if more. A second species family segment is "a list of arc family segments; a value of C", on the interval where the C-intervals of the arc segments intersect; "for each value of C there is only one orbit".
- Bifurcation orbit (section 6.0, p.95): Definition 6.0.1 "A branch is a part of a family of generating orbits emanating in a given direction from a given generating orbit." Proposition 6.0.1 "The number of branches emanating from a generating orbit is always even." (Not proved; "no exception has been found in hundreds of examined cases".) Definition 6.0.2 "A bifurcation orbit is a generating orbit from which more than two branches emerge." A bifurcation orbit is TOTAL if all deflection angles vanish (the whole orbit lies on one supporting ellipse) and PARTIAL if one or more do not (p.98). Bifurcations are of type 1, 2 or 3 after the type of the ellipse, with the short name <type><T or P><order>, for example 2T3.

## 2. The machinery for the arcs (READ pp.42-56, 68-73) and what I checked

### 2.1 Elements of the supporting ellipse (eqs. 4.9 to 4.19, READ pp.43-44)

With A = a^(3/2) (eq. 4.10, "equal to the period of the elliptic motion divided by 2 pi"), the collision at P with velocity (V, gamma): a = 1/(2 - V^2), A = (2 - V^2)^(-3/2), co-eccentricity e' = V sqrt(2 - V^2) cos gamma (eq. 4.12), e = sqrt(1 - V^2 (2 - V^2) cos^2 gamma) (eq. 4.13). Inverse (4.19): V = sqrt(2 - 1/a). Time of passage at P is t = 0. Passages of M2 through Q occur at t2 = t20 + 2 pi alpha and of M4 through Q at t4 = t40 + 2 pi A beta, alpha and beta integers (eqs. 4.20, 4.23), with the offsets t20 and t40 of eqs. 4.22 and 4.24-4.25. An S-arc exists when t2 = t4, which is (eq. 4.27) Z = beta A - alpha with, from the page image (eq. 4.28):
pi Z = (1/2)(t20 - t40) = -sign(cos gamma) arccos[(1 - V^2 cos^2 gamma)/sqrt(1 - V^2 (2 - V^2) cos^2 gamma)] - (2 - V^2)^(-3/2) arctan[V sqrt(2 - V^2) sin gamma/(V^2 - 1)] + V sin gamma/(2 - V^2).
"A is a definite function of V ... for any given pair of integers alpha, beta, (4.27) and (4.28) define a relation between V and gamma, which is the equation of a S-arc family" (p.47). Equivalence to Henon 1968 relation (30) and to Perko's timing condition (eq. 4.29, notation table). Midpoint on the x axis at x = -a (-1)^alpha [(-1)^beta (1 - 2 H(1 - A)) - e] (eq. 4.38; text layer only, not checked on the page image).

### 2.2 The (A, Z) plane: the enumeration of single arcs (READ pp.48-59)

- Every S-arc family is a straight line Z = beta A - alpha in the (A, Z) plane (eq. 4.27) inside the domain D = D1 + D2+ + D2- + D3 (eq. 4.44): D1 is A > 1, -1 < Z < 1 (eq. 4.40); D2 is 2^(-3/2) < A < 1, 0 < Z <= Zm(A) with two sheets D2+ and D2- sewn along the curved boundary Gamma (eq. 4.41); D3 is the segment A = 1, -1 < Z < 0 (eq. 4.43). "The straight-line characteristics of the (A, Z) plane correspond thus to sinusoids in the (N, e*) plane (Bruno 1973)" (p.52).
- Existence (eq. 4.48): an arc family S_{alpha,beta} exists for any pair of integers (alpha, beta) with (beta = 0 and alpha = 0) or (beta > 0 and alpha > 2^(-3/2) beta) or (beta < 0 and alpha < 2^(-3/2) beta). Ten cases of the line against the domains (Fig. 4.10, Table 4.1, p.56).
- T-arc families: A = I/J (eq. 4.54) with I, J positive and mutually prime, which is a vertical line in D1 (I > J), in D2 (I < J, existing only when I/J > 2^(-3/2), eq. 4.55) or in D3 (I = J = 1); every point stands for two T-arcs, ingoing (PP) and outgoing (QQ). "Proposition 4.3.2. An ordinary generating orbit of the second species cannot contain two identical T-arcs of type 1 in succession." (p.56; a heuristic perturbation argument is given, pp.56-57, eqs. 4.50-4.53: "Neighbouring orbits cannot be constructed for small mu, and therefore the orbit Q0 is not generating"; the rigorous proof is deferred to the sequel.) Consequence (p.57): I and J must be mutually prime for a T-arc.
- Types 2 to 4 (section 4.4, pp.59-62): type 2 arcs are isolated: a = (I*/J*)^(2/3) with I*, J* any integers, I* not equal to J*, e = |a - 1|/a (eq. 4.58), duration 2 pi I*, m basic arcs where m is the greatest common divisor, motion direct (gamma = 0) or retrograde (gamma = pi). Type 3: the retrograde unit circle, a half-circle per basic arc, C = -1. Type 4: the direct unit circle, "M4 coincides with M2 at all times"; C = 3, so a type 4 arc forces the whole orbit to be third species.
- Critical arcs, Table 4.4 (section 4.6, pp.68-73): "an arc corresponding to an extremum of C inside an arc family will be called a critical arc (Hitzl and Henon 1977a, 1977b)". For an S-arc family the condition is the tangency of the straight characteristic to a curve C = constant (eq. 4.80, with the slope formula eq. 4.79, which I did not transcribe) and "can only be solved numerically; solutions have been tabulated by Hitzl and Henon (1977a) for all arc families with -9 <= alpha <= 9, -9 <= beta <= 9". "It turns out that, for each of the cases distinguished on Fig. 4.10, there is a fixed number of critical arcs ... This fact was first conjectured by Bruno (1973), then supported by the numerical results of Hitzl and Henon (1977a), and finally proved by Bruno (1978b)." The numbers (Table 4.1 last column, read with Fig. 4.10): case 1: 1; case 2: 1; case 3: 0; case 4: 0; case 5: 1; case 6: 2; case 7: 2; case 8: 2; case 9: 0; case 10: 0. A T-arc family has none: A is constant, C is monotonic in gamma. Each arc family is cut by its critical arcs into one, two or three segments S-, S0, S+ (Fig. 4.10).

## 3. How a generating orbit relates to periodic orbits at small positive mass

1. Patched conics, stated as a conjecture and as standard practice (section 4.7, p.74), verbatim: "(1) All deflection angles are different from zero. It seems reasonable to conjecture that in this case, neighbouring orbits can be generated for mu not equal to 0, by means of a 'patched conics' procedure: each collision point is replaced by a small arc of hyperbolic orbit near M2, the distance to M2 being adjusted to produce the correct deflection angle; and each arc between collisions is slightly modified so as to meet the hyperbolic arcs at both ends. This procedure can be applied iteratively. The method is standard practice in astronautics, and works well; convergence is fast for small values of mu, and the patched conics orbit is a good approximation to an exact orbit. However, today there is no mathematical proof in the general case that an ordinary orbit of the second species is generating. Proofs have been achieved in particular cases, notably by Perko (1974, 1976b) and Henrard (1980) for symmetric orbits with a small number of arcs p." Hénon gives no hyperbola formulas; the first-order relation is in Gomez & Olle (the project's gate relation) and Perko.
2. Zero deflection (p.74): "(ii) One or more deflection angles are zero. In that case, the answer is not so simple. The above intuitive approach fails, because for mu not equal to 0 it is impossible to devise an arc of hyperbola giving a zero deflection angle. The orbit is definitely not generating in certain cases; according to Proposition 4.3.2, an ordinary generating orbit of the second species cannot contain two identical T-arcs of type 1 in succession." Conjecture 4.7.1: "An orbit of the second species which does not contain two identical T-arcs in succession is a generating orbit." (p.74; proofs for particular cases by Perko 1977a, 1981a.)
3. Ordinary orbit versus bifurcation orbit: the family of generating orbits is displaced by O(mu) at an ordinary orbit; at a bifurcation orbit the branches join in a new way, decided by two invariants: symmetry (chapter 7: symmetric orbits stay symmetric, a symmetric branch cannot join an asymmetric one) and Broucke's principle (chapter 8, READ pp.137-139): "Roughly speaking, it states that the side of passage of M3 with respect to M2 is invariant." With sigma = sign(delta theta cos theta) (eq. 8.5), delta theta the deflection angle (eq. 8.4, theta_a - theta_b between the arc after and the arc before the collision, small near a bifurcation), and in a total bifurcation of order n there are n invariants. For an S-arc theta' = -theta at the end (symmetry), for a T-arc theta' = theta (p.139). "Symmetry is obviously a weak criterion: junctions have been established in one case only" (7.3.3, p.134). "Broucke's principle is powerful ... It is not sufficient, however, to establish junctions in general; the number of branches always grows more rapidly than the number of subsets." (8.6, p.167). So in general the branch junction at a bifurcation orbit is NOT determined by the qualitative theory; the quantitative analysis is deferred to the unpublished sequel.
4. First-order size of the bifurcation neighbourhood: not given in the book beyond "no longer simply of the order of mu".
5. Comparison with computed families at the Earth-Moon mass (chapter 10.4, pp.220-233), the only place the book meets mu = 0.012155: the nine natural families a, b, c, f, g, h, i, l, m (Stromgren's names), computed by Broucke 1968 for mu_EM = 0.012155, against the generating families built by the algorithm of 10.1. Results: "Figs. 10.1 to 10.11 show that the families of periodic orbits for mu = mu_EM = 0.012155 are close to the generating families in most cases. A divergence is observed only for family i, and then only after a common initial part (Fig. 10.7)." (10.5, p.232). Family i: "the characteristics diverge ... at the fifth accident, i.e. the bifurcation ... Thus, the value mu_EM = 0.012155 of the Earth-Moon case is sufficiently different from 0 to produce large-scale changes in the family of periodic orbits." (p.225). Same family at the Sun-Jupiter mass 0.00095388 follows the generating family for longer and diverges at the fifteenth accident (p.229), and "There should exist a sequence of critical values of mu, possibly infinite, at each of which the behaviour of the family of periodic orbits becomes qualitatively different." (p.229). Cosmetic difference noted for family a (p.221): "the angular points in Fig. 10.1b are replaced by rounded corners in Fig. 10.1a" (the Earth-Moon curve is the smoothed generating curve), plus a bulge of third-species type of size mu^(2/3), about 0.05 in C and mu^(1/3), about 0.23 in x, at mu_EM (p.222). Extrapolation, stated as a belief, not shown (10.5, p.232): "Clearly, the agreement observed in the above figures for mu = mu_EM should hold for all values in the interval 0 < mu < mu_EM." and "As an extreme example ... this oscillating structure ... is apparently still present at mu = 0.5 ... Finally, going to the limit mu going to 1 ..." (a case where the generating-family analysis "in fact explains the structure of a family of periodic orbits for all values of the mass ratio"). The comparison is qualitative (characteristics in the (C, x) plane); no individual orbits are compared (p.232: "This is not done here because it would take too much space").
6. The comparison covers the nine natural families only, which begin as very small or very large circular orbits or at a Lagrange point. It does not test the resonant flyby cyclers (Casoliva type) and does not include any family with a close approach below the Moon's radius. INFERRED limitation.

## 4. Techniques applicable to the project's problems

### (a) Enumeration of generating orbits and families, precise enough to code, and its relation to `#899`

The enumeration at a fixed Jacobi constant (the form `#899` needs, since a seed is a (p, q, C) triple):
1. Fix C less than 3 (v^2 = 3 - C, eq. 8.3). Elliptic arcs need C greater than -2 sqrt 2 (eq. 4.8); hyperbolic arcs (family Sh) cover C less than Cp = -0.720283; type 3 needs C = -1; type 4 needs C = 3.
2. Alphabet of S-arcs: for each integer pair (alpha, beta) with eq. 4.48 true, intersect the line Z = beta A - alpha with the domain D (Fig. 4.10, Table 4.1) and with the level set C(A, Z) = C; each arc-family segment, being monotonic in C (section 4.6), contributes at most one arc. Solve for gamma from Z (eq. 4.28, unique in D1 and D3; two-valued in D2, one per sheet), then V from eq. 4.12, then C from eq. 4.6 to pick the point on the line.
3. Alphabet of T-arcs: A = I/J, I and J coprime (eq. 4.54), V from A, gamma from eq. 4.6 at the given C (C is monotonic in gamma along the vertical line); two arcs, PP and QQ. Type 2 arcs are isolated points, type 3 only at C = -1.
4. Every arc carries: its duration 2 tau (eq. 4.2; 2 pi I for a T-arc), the entry velocity (V, gamma) and so the rotating-frame (v, theta) by eq. 8.2, and the exit velocity (theta' = -theta for an S-arc, theta' = theta for a T-arc). Both are COMPUTED-confirmed below.
5. Chain: a periodic word u1 u2 ... up of arcs all at the same C; the orbit closes automatically (all collisions are at M2, all arcs share C), the period is the sum of the durations; the only forbidden words are those with two identical T-arcs in succession (Proposition 4.3.2); Conjecture 4.7.1: all others are generating orbits. Cyclic permutations are the same orbit; a word that is a power of a shorter word is not minimal.
6. Demanded turn at junction j: delta theta = theta_(j+1) - theta'_j (eq. 8.4), reduced to an angle in (0, pi); delta theta = 0 means the two arcs lie on the same supporting ellipse (a bifurcation junction, not an encounter), and by eq. 4.6 delta theta = pi means head-on.
7. Seed at mu > 0: patched conics, a hyperbola at each junction with the deflection angle delta theta (the first-order periapsis relation is the project's gate relation and Gomez & Olle's); the corrections are in the other papers (Barrabes & Gomez seeds, Casoliva continuation).
8. Families: move C; each arc slides along its characteristic; when an arc passes a critical arc (Table 4.4) it turns back (section 4.9, Fig. 4.19); when two or more arcs reach critical arcs at the same C the continuation is undetermined (Restriction 4.9.1: "The cases where two or more arcs reach an extremum simultaneously will not be considered").
9. Bifurcations along a chain: monitor the deflection angles; two successive arcs share a supporting ellipse iff they are elliptic, are the same point of the (A, Z) domain (same sheet in D2) and the first ends where the second begins (section 9.3.1.3, p.180); for two S-arcs on lines Z = beta1 A - alpha1 and Z = beta2 A - alpha2 the common point is A = (alpha2 - alpha1)/(beta2 - beta1), Z = (beta1 alpha2 - beta2 alpha1)/(beta2 - beta1) (eqs. 9.5, 9.6); then I/J = A in lowest terms and L = Z J must be in Table 6.2 or 6.3 (section 9.3.1.3). Non-basic arcs (an arc that passes through M2 inside itself) are the other source of partial bifurcations (9.3.1.4, rules 9.9 to 9.12).
Relation to Barrabes and Gomez. Hénon enumerates the mu = 0 skeleton (arcs and chains at fixed C), Barrabes and Gomez give seed formulas at small positive mu for the single-arc p-q case, Casoliva continues to the physical mass. The labelling: Hénon's I counts the Moon's revolutions and J the particle's (a = (I/J)^(2/3)), so in the Barrabes and Gomez labelling (p revolutions of the particle, q of the Moon) it is (p, q) = (J, I). COMPUTED check of that mapping in the digest of the 1977 paper: the critical arcs C12, C23, C37 are the Casoliva resonances 2-1, 3-2, 7-3 (q = I, p = J) and the printed Jacobi constants of the Casoliva rows lie inside the C interval spanned by the two critical arcs of the family. The type 1 bifurcation ellipses of Table 6.2 and 6.3 are the p-q resonant orbits on which the arcs degenerate to zero deflection; INFERRED: they are where Barrabes and Gomez's matching degenerates (their nondegeneracy condition).
What the book adds to `#899` beyond P2: (i) the list of all arcs at a given C, so a chain search at the Earth-Moon C values is an enumeration, not a search; (ii) the exclusion rule for repeated T-arcs; (iii) the count of critical arcs per family, which gives the number of C-folds a family must have: a check on the continuation driver. INFERRED and untested: Casoliva's "four family segments for the seed 7-3 resonance" might be compared with the arcs near (I, J) = (3, 7); I did not examine that.

### (b) The test for critical generating orbits, and the demanded-turn gate

- In this book a critical ARC is one with an extremum of C along its family (eq. 4.80, Table 4.4); a critical ORBIT is a periodic orbit with z = +1 or -1 (section 2.8). The link (Propositions 2.8.1 and 2.8.2): z = +1 at an extremum of C or at an intersection with another family. For the second species the 1977 paper proves the other direction: the orbit's stability is infinite for ordinary orbits and the critical generating orbits are the extrema of C (see that digest, section 2).
- A test for a chain: it reaches a fold in C (a turning point of the continuation) exactly when one of its arcs is a critical arc (an arc whose characteristic is tangent to the C level set), which is eq. 4.80 on each arc separately; for the arcs with |alpha|, |beta| at most 5 it is Table 4.4, for at most 9 it is the 1977 Table I. A continuation driver in C should expect the fold count per arc of Table 4.1 (0, 1 or 2). INFERRED.
- Relation to the risk that a demanded-turn gate may wrongly reject a chain at a bifurcation orbit. What the book says: at a bifurcation orbit one or more deflection angles are zero (total bifurcation: all of them; partial: some); the two arcs are then on the same supporting ellipse, the collision is not a real encounter ("the orbit then goes through M2 as if M2 did not exist", p.36), and the branches there are not simply displaced. So a gate that treats "turn of 0 is not an encounter" is consistent with the book at those junctions, but it has two consequences that the project should handle explicitly (INFERRED):
  1. The zero-turn junction between two DIFFERENT arcs on the same ellipse (S-arc followed by an S-arc, or an S-arc and a T-arc) is a bifurcation orbit, not an invalid chain. Such chains are legitimate generating orbits whose family structure at positive mass is decided by Broucke's principle and symmetry. The gate should report them as "bifurcation: zero turn on a shared ellipse" and keep them, not reject.
  2. The zero-turn junction between two IDENTICAL T-arcs is excluded for positive mass (Proposition 4.3.2, a heuristic proof): a chain that repeats a resonant return leg with no turn is not a limit of ballistic chains. The gate may reject it with that reason (INFERRED rule; the book proves it only heuristically).
  Concrete check to add to `src/cyclerfinder/verify/turn_gate.py` (not coded under this task): for each junction compute the demanded turn from rotating-frame velocities (the book's definition, eq. 8.4); if it is below the encounter tolerance, test whether the incoming leg and the outgoing leg lie on the same Kepler ellipse (the same a, e and orientation to a tolerance, which is the book's same-point-of-(A, Z) condition) and whether the leg is a T-arc (duration equal to 2 pi I and a equal to (I/J)^(2/3) for coprime I, J); then return one of: "no encounter (zero turn, different ellipses)", "bifurcation junction (zero turn, same ellipse, S or mixed arcs): keep, do not rank", "identical T-arcs: not generating for positive mass (Proposition 4.3.2): reject". Also report, for every junction, the validity numbers mu |ln mu| / v^3 and r_p / sqrt(mu) as proposed in the 1977 digest, since at small v the book's own comparisons are at mu of 1e-3 to 1e-2 only.
- COMPUTED consistency check of the turn: for five Table 4.4 arcs (A0(-1), A0(1), A1(-1), B1(-1), A1(-2)) I took (A, Z), got (V, gamma), then theta = atan2(V sin gamma, V cos gamma - 1) from eq. 8.2. The orbit made of that single S-arc has delta theta = theta - theta' = 2 theta (eq. 8.4 with theta' = -theta). Reduced to (0, pi) this gives 65.09, 1.765, 15.572, 16.300 and 11.605 degrees. The same turns, to the printed digits, come from the 1977 paper's fixed-axes geometry (see that digest, section 5(b)), a different route. So the book's (v, theta) definition and the 1977 recipe agree, and the book's definition can be used as the independent expected value for the gate on a single-arc orbit.

### (c) More than one close approach per period; the Earth-Moon mass ratio; two small bodies

- More than one close approach per period: this is the book's general case (composite orbits of p arcs; "Note that there can be more than one collision per period", p.35). The book's chain machinery (sections 4.7, 4.8, 9.3.3) is therefore the only source in the set that treats chains. Its results are: the zero-turn rules above, the C-interval condition for a list of arc segments (the intersection of the arcs' C intervals must be non-empty), the continuation rule at an extremum, and the restriction that two or more arcs reaching a critical arc at the same C are left out. It also records that the generic number of branches at a total bifurcation of order n grows quickly (Appendix C, not read). Marco and Niederman (1995) are cited for the density of composite orbits made of two S-arcs (p.74); that paper is digested in the repository (`docs/notes/CORPUS_INDEX.md`).
- Earth-Moon mass ratio: section 3 point 5 above. The only mu_EM material is the nine natural families; the book's claim that generating-family structure persists to mu_EM is a belief supported by eleven comparisons and one divergence (family i), not a theorem. Nothing in the book is about resonant lunar-flyby cyclers.
- Two small bodies: not treated. The book is "the classical circular restricted problem of three bodies" only (p.38); the elliptic extension is cited to Gomez and Olle 1986, 1991a, b and Howell 1987, Howell and Marsh 1991, "But their results will not be used in the present monograph".

## 5. Open questions, quoted

- "However, today there is no mathematical proof in the general case that an ordinary orbit of the second species is generating." (p.74). Conjecture 4.7.1 stays a conjecture; Proposition 6.0.1 (even number of branches) is "supported empirically".
- "Thus, the whole problem is incredibly rich in its details." and "A planned sequel to the present volume will describe a more ambitious attack of the problem, consisting in a quantitative analysis of the families in the vicinity of a bifurcation orbit." (p.4). The sequel is not cited in the reference list.
- "One hope which has not been realized is finding, via the generating families, a systematic classification of periodic orbits and their families. No governing principle, no particular order can be discerned in the structure of the generating families" (10.5, p.233).
- Simultaneous extrema (Restriction 4.9.1) are excluded; the book gives one exception by symmetry (family a).
