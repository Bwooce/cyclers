## What the paper is
Abstract (quoted, in part): "A detailed trade space analysis of the proposed Uranus Orbiter and Probe
mission informs options related to decisions on science, cost, and risk, including design choices of
science tour phases, probe and orbiter payload mass, orbiter spacecraft mass, mission duration, and
frequency of launch opportunities." "A broad search of both interplanetary cruise trajectories and
Uranian tours produce a trade space of launch opportunities, flight times, and DeltaV that set bounds
on spacecraft dry and wet mass." The concept uses a Falcon Heavy and a chemical propulsion system;
"Gravity assists from Venus and/or Earth enable launches throughout the 2030s, when Jupiter is
unavailable." The approach "would deliver a probe to Uranus and completes a tour of its rings, moons,
and magnetosphere within 19 years. A mission duration of 21 years enables multiple close approaches to
Uranus".

Sections (as printed): 1 Introduction; 2 Science Objectives and Mission Drivers (Atmospheres; Rings and
Small Satellites; Large Moons; Magnetospheres; Interiors; Table 1); 3 Example Tour Phases (UOI and
Probe Relay; Moon Tour; Magneto-Tour; Uranus Close Approaches); 4 Uranian Tour Trade Space; 5
Interplanetary Cruise Trade Space; 6 UOP Spacecraft Mass Model; 7 Mission Trade Space; 8 Conclusions
and Future Work; Appendices A (Launch Vehicle Performance), B (Launch Opportunities), C (Earth Flyby
Opportunities). Nine tables and 20 figures.

Mission phases of the example tour (Sec. 3 and Figure 8 title: "Example tour comprising Probe Relay,
Moon Tour, Magneto-Tour, and Uranus Close Passes"): (1) Uranus orbit insertion (UOI) and probe relay
(120-day capture orbit, minimum periapsis 26,559 km, UOI in the gap between the epsilon and nu rings,
probe released from orbit, 60-minute descent to 10 bar); (2) moon tour of the five large moons, rings
and small satellites; (3) magneto-tour, which rotates the petal (apoapsis) "using Titania gravity
assists" to sample the magnetosphere and "has an added benefit of rotating the orbit so that Uranus
close approach becomes visible from Earth"; (4) a "Juno-like sequence of orbits [12] optimized for the
interiors objectives" (Uranus close passes below 1.1 Uranus radii, Figure 7: "10 orbits with 2000-km
periapsis and 28-day period"). Table 1 (mission drivers): large moons baseline "3 flybys per moon",
threshold "2 flybys per moon"; two encounters are "distinct" if close-approach positions are separated
by more than 45 deg or sub-solar points by more than 60 deg; baseline flybys are "(<100 km)".

## The moon tour (Sec. 3, "Moon Tour")
Goal and method, quoted: "During the moon tour phase, the overarching goal is to maximize the variety
of observations of Uranus' large moons. The design approach is to first develop a large trade space of
potential tours using the patched-conics algorithm detailed in Ref. [13], then filter on quantifiable
parameters derived from the science objectives. The algorithm builds tours by constraining close
approach to a minimum allowable altitude (50 km) and checking whether the flyby occurs on an inbound
(decreasing distance from Uranus) or outbound node."

- Rationale for low altitude: "A low altitude is preferred to detect variations in the moon's gravity
  field and to search for a potential ocean via magnetic induction. A variety of inbound and outbound
  encounters ... provide a variety of lighting conditions for imaging. The design algorithm also
  preferentially seeks tours with a variety of ground tracks, as indicated in Figure 4."
- Ref. [13] is printed as D. Landau, A. Davis and R. Karimi, "Trajectory Options for a Uranus Orbiter
  and Probe," AAS/AIAA Astrodynamics Specialist Conference, Big Sky, MT, 2023 (full entry in the
  bibliography below). The paper gives no further description of the algorithm in this text; no moon
  ephemeris, dynamical model, resonance logic or search statistics are stated. The filters named are
  "quantifiable parameters derived from the science objectives" (distinct encounters per moon,
  latitude, longitude, lighting, phase) with no numeric filter thresholds beyond Table 1 and the 50 km
  altitude.
- The example tour is "a particular sequence of orbits that satisfy the baseline objectives of all
  science disciplines". Table 3 is "the example moon tour"; Figure 4 states "The tour from Figure 3
  comprises 3 flybys of Miranda, 7 of Ariel, 4 of Umbriel, 8 of Titania, and 4 of Oberon." Figure 3's
  labels read "Oberon (4 flybys), Titania (8), Umbriel (4), Ariel (7), Miranda (3)" (the figure is
  captioned "A variety of encounter phases and latitude / longitude of close approach support
  moon-tour science objectives").
- Ref. [14] (Landau, Campagnola, Pellegrini, "Star Searches for Patched-Conic Trajectories") is used
  in Sec. 5 for the interplanetary cruise search ("employing the Star patched-conics algorithm [14]"),
  not named for the moon tour.

## Trade-space material (Sec. 4 onwards)
- Sec. 4 (Uranian tour trade space, Figures 8 and 9): tours are traded on science scenario against
  delta-v and duration. "Tours that only include probe relay and encounters of the large moons require
  the least amount of delta-v and flight time"; adding magneto-tours "extends flight time by about 2
  years without significantly increasing delta-v"; extra delta-v is needed to change plane, lower
  periapsis for Uranus close passes and for disposal into Uranus. UOI inside the rings gives "a net
  reduction in total delta-v of ~0.2-0.3 km/s". The example full tour of Figure 8 is starred in Figure
  9 (the delta-v and duration axes are in the figure, not stated in text).
- Sec. 5 (interplanetary cruise): searched with the Star patched-conic algorithm [14] using Venus, Earth
  and/or Mars gravity assists (no Jupiter, which is "not available for launches in 2034-2041");
  Venus-assist cruises have lower C3, perihelion 0.60-0.72 au; Earth-only cruises have shorter flight
  times, lower delta-v, higher C3, perihelia above 0.9 au; statistical delta-v and margin 450-550 m/s
  (Table 4). Sequences in Appendix B are of EVEEU, EVVEEU, EVVEU and EVVVEU form.
- Sec. 6 (mass model): UOP core mass about 1000 kg (280 kg probe, 120 kg instruments, 600 kg bus);
  15 percent launch margin, 25 percent dry margin, propulsion plus structures 30 percent of
  propellant, Isp 320 s; delta-v "approaching 3 km/s" for both science tour and cruise.
- Sec. 7 (mission trade space): a 2,200 kg predicted dry mass design (up to 4,000 kg propellant,
  maximum wet mass 6,750 kg, total delta-v about 2.8 km/s) is chosen; mission duration is 17 yr
  average and 17.5 yr maximum for moon tours (1.5 yr tour, 15.5 yr cruise), 21.5 yr for full tours;
  19 launch opportunities for moon tours and 13 for full tours over eight years; moon-tour delta-v
  (cruise plus UOI deterministic and statistical, delta-v1) about 1.8-2.0 km/s with tour delta-v2 about
  0.73-0.78 km/s (Table 7); moon plus magneto-tour mission 18-19.5 yr (Table 8); full tour 19.7-21.5 yr
  (Table 9). Core mass growth of 100 kg costs about 2 months with Venus flybys, about 6 months for
  Earth-only. Figure 17 shows 20.8 yr maximum for the full tour and 16.8 yr for the moon-only tour
  after a one-launch-per-year cadence is imposed.
- Sec. 8 conclusions: baseline objectives for atmospheres, moons, rings and magnetospheres "would be
  accomplished in less than 19 years from launch"; interiors adds 20 years (threshold) or 21 years
  (full baseline).

## Coordinator's verdict for the six Uranian quasi_cycler rows (added 2026-10-03, `#869`)

A judgement by the coordinating session, written after reading the tour material directly;
everything above is the factual digest.

**No periodic or cycler trajectory is published here, but the example tour ends with the nearest
published geometry to the project's flagship row.**

- The paper builds a large trade space of one-off tours with a patched-conic algorithm (its Ref.
  [13]) and never uses cycler, periodic, free-return, resonant or repeating about a trajectory.
- Rows 23-26 of Table 3 alternate Umbriel, Oberon, Umbriel, Oberon at flight times 648.4, 674.1,
  683.2 and 703.5 days (intervals 25.7, 9.1 and 20.3 days), with V-infinity 4.0, 2.7, 4.1 and
  2.6 km/s. The V-infinity nearly repeats from lap to lap. It is the end of the table, so the
  alternation is two laps long and is not discussed by the authors.
- The catalogued `umbriel-oberon-1-1-uranian-quasi-cycler-2026` is the same moon pair in a
  different regime: equal legs of 14.94 days, V-infinity 0.92 / 0.96 / 0.89 km/s, repeating over
  epoch windows. The published stretch has unequal intervals and V-infinity three to four times
  higher at Umbriel.
- Together with the sibling paper (AAS 25-668, an Oberon-Ariel-Oberon-Ariel stretch), two of the
  six catalogued pairs now have a published one-off alternating stretch in the Uranus Orbiter and
  Probe tour literature. All tour V-infinity values in both papers are 2.6-4.5 km/s; the six rows
  sit at 0.89-2.16 km/s.

**Consequence proposed (owner's call, not applied):** keep `candidate-novel`; cite this paper on
the Umbriel-Oberon row and AAS 25-668 on the Ariel-Oberon row as nearest published geometry; and
describe the rows as "no published repeating or periodic two-moon trajectory, and none in this
V-infinity regime" rather than "no published two-moon geometry".

**One reference still unread:** this paper's Ref. [13], Landau, Davis & Karimi, "Trajectory
Options for a Uranus Orbiter and Probe", AAS/AIAA Astrodynamics Specialist Conference, Big Sky,
2023, which holds the tour algorithm and possibly further example tours. It is not in the corpus.
