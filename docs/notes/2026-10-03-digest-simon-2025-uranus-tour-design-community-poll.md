# Digest — Simon et al. (2025), "Uranus Flagship Science-Driven Tour Design: Community Input Poll" (arXiv:2505.05514)

**Digested:** 2026-10-03 (9 pages; text layer plus all nine pages viewed as images, because the
poll questions and results are figures). **Citation:** Amy Simon (NASA GSFC), Ian Cohen (Johns
Hopkins University Applied Physics Lab), Matthew Hedman (University of Idaho), Mark Hofstadter
(JPL), Kathleen Mandt (NASA GSFC), Francis Nimmo (UC Santa Cruz), "Uranus Flagship
Science-Driven Tour Design: Community Input Poll," arXiv:2505.05514 (2025). Filed in the private
paper corpus as
`simon-cohen-hedman-hofstadter-mandt-nimmo-2025-uranus-flagship-science-driven-tour-design-community-input-poll-arxiv-2505.05514.pdf`
(9 pages, md5 `91fb3ed9bd7fc04379271f30b507546d`). **Corpus index description (one line):**
Community poll (72 responses, Jan-Mar 2025) of science-driven tour requirements for the Uranus
Orbiter and Probe, by science Objective: phase angle, range, inclination, cadence, coverage,
tour length, moon coverage and priority; no trajectories.

## What it is
A short grassroots report, "a community-led effort with no institutional or NASA sponsorship",
whose results are "not binding nor decisional in any way". Context: the Uranus Orbiter and Probe
(UOP) is the highest-priority Planetary Flagship of the 2023-2032 decadal survey [1]; the
decadal design study's tour was "a best effort" including orbit insertion, probe release and
relay, and multiple satellite flybys, and "could not be perfectly optimized" [2]; more recent
work [3,4] (Landau et al. 2023; Ellison et al. 2024) shows the tour "can be modified to meet
individual science disciplines in a much more efficient manner, particularly for the satellite
flybys". Table 1 of the paper reproduces the UOP science Objectives and measurements from [2]
(Atmospheres A1-A3; Interiors I1-I4; Magnetosphere M1-M3; Rings and Small Satellites R1-R2;
Large Satellites and Ocean Worlds S1-S4).

## What was polled
- Run 3 January to 30 March 2025; notices to planetary-science mailing lists and posted at the
  Lunar and Planetary Science Conference. **72 responses**, "the majority for satellite
  Objectives"; none for Objectives I4 or M1. Respondents picked one Objective (several
  responses allowed) and ranked aspects of a potential UOP orbital tour on a 1 (lowest) to 5
  (highest) scale; all questions optional.
- Questions (Figs. 1-2, read from the images; small print): observation types (remote
  sensing of Uranus, major satellites, rings and small moons; gravity passes of Uranus or
  satellites; fields and particles; radio occultations; in situ probe); dedicated science orbits
  by target; target illumination (low phase = Sun incidence angle under 45 degrees, high phase
  over 90 degrees, mid, mixed); target range (planet or magnetosphere: distant 20+ radii to as
  close as possible under about 1.5 radii; satellites and rings: distant 100,000+ km to as close
  as possible 10,000 km or less); **flyby inclination** (low = equatorial to high = polar,
  mixed); spatial coverage and resolution; temporal coverage (short duration under 17 hours,
  long over 17 hours; low cadence = separated by months or longer, high cadence = minutes to
  days); **orbital coverage for satellite gravity** (all major moons single pass each; all
  major moons, some with multiple passes; some moons, multiple inclinations; one moon, many
  passes or geometries); magnetosphere local-time coverage; **moon priority** if only one major
  satellite could be favoured (Ariel, Miranda, Oberon, Titania, Umbriel); tour length and number
  of targeted observations (fewer than 5 or 5 or more over 2 or 4 years, or a mission longer
  than 4 years; the question assumes "orbits last 30-40 days (~10 orbits per year)"); interest
  in future activities.
- Aggregation (Figs. 3-10): Objectives with more than six responses individually; those with
  fewer than six grouped with the closest Objective: A3/I1 with I2 and I3; R1 with R2; M2 with
  M3; S2 with S4 (A1, A2, S1, S3 individual). Percentages are normalised by responses to the
  Objective, so "highest priority" votes do not always total 100 percent.

## Results that constrain a moon tour
Stated in the text (Cross-Objective Comparisons):
- "While respondents for all three [satellite] Objectives show the preference for at least one
  flyby of each of the five major moons, **Ariel is the highest priority target for detailed
  study, though Miranda is also of high interest**."
- Figure 12 colours any characteristic named "highest" by at least 50 percent of respondents
  (blue) or "high" or "highest" by at least 50 percent (pink); the paper says it is "informative
  to see if all disciplines' highest priorities can be achieved with a single tour" and compares
  the approach to Cassini's orbit-segment allocation among disciplines. The paper gives no
  verdict on whether a single tour satisfies all.

Read from the bar charts (Figs. 8-11), approximate and not stated in the text; a figure-reading,
not a transcription:
- Satellite coverage preference (Fig. 11, top row): for S1, S2/S4 and S3 the largest "highest
  priority" bar is "all major moons, some with multiple passes", about 70 percent in each.
- Highest-priority major satellite (Fig. 11, bottom row): S1 Ariel about 57 percent, a second bar
  about 29 percent (moon not reliably identified from the colour key); S2/S4 Ariel and Miranda
  about 38 percent each, Umbriel about 15 percent; S3 Ariel about 53 percent, Miranda about 40
  percent, Umbriel about 7 percent.
- Satellite Objectives (S1, S2/S4, S3): "as close as possible" is the largest highest-priority
  range bar, about 67 to 72 percent; global coverage with mixed resolution is the largest
  highest-priority coverage bar, about 43 to 80 percent. Inclination preferences are spread
  across low, high and mixed. These are for the polled target distance and coverage
  categories defined above (satellite range of 10,000 km or less), not flyby altitudes in km.
- No question or result gives a flyby altitude for any moon, a number of flybys per moon, or a
  tour duration beyond the "tour length" bins above.

## Does it contain trajectory content
Counts (text layer; figure text is image and was read by eye): "cycler" 0; "periodic" 0;
"repeating" 0; "resonan" 0; "V-infinity" 0; "altitude" 0; "inclin" 0 in the text layer (the
word "inclination" appears only in the figure questions and chart titles); "flyby" 3;
"trajectory" or "tour" 27. There is **no trajectory, no tour sequence, no encounter
sequence, no V-infinity value, no resonance or orbital period, no orbit design** and no delta-V.
Orbit "durations" appear only as the poll's assumption of 30-40 day orbits (about ten orbits per
year) in a question and as the bins of tour length (2 years, 4 years, over 4 years).

## What it does NOT contain
- Any trajectory, tour itinerary, flyby altitude, V-infinity, resonance, cycler, periodic or
  repeating orbit, mass or power or data-volume budget.
- A statement of how many flybys per moon are wanted beyond "at least one flyby of each of the
  five major moons" and "some with multiple passes" as the most favoured option.
- Orbit-inclination values in degrees (only low, middle, high and mixed categories) or
  sun-phase results as numbers in the text.
- Statistics beyond bar-chart percentages; the raw and aggregated data are on Zenodo
  (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15342504), not in the paper.
- The 72 responses are a self-selected sample at a conference audience; the paper itself says
  the number of responses is not indicative of science priority.
