## What the paper is
A concept-update paper for the Decadal-survey UOP mission. Abstract (quoted): "With revised assumptions since the Decadal,
we have explored a large trade space including launch vehicles, propulsion options, cruise trajectories, available power
sources, viable concept of operations, and science data return for later launch dates without a Jupiter gravity assist. The
most repeatable trajectory solutions employ either a commercially derived solar electric propulsion (SEP) transfer stage or
the availability of a more capable launch vehicle under development, such as the SpaceX Starship. Orbit insertion has been
moved farther from Uranus to acknowledge the remaining uncertainty in Uranian ring structure." It also states a two-Next-Gen-RTG
orbiter and "a detailed concept of operations for three representative science cases, returning 13-15 Gbit of science data and
spacecraft telemetry per ~34 day orbit."

Sections: 1 Introduction; 2 Uranus Orbiter Mission Challenges (2.1 Reaching the Uranus System; 2.2 System Arrival and Orbit
Insertion; 2.3 Uranus System Tour and Resource Limitations); 3 Updated Trajectory Trade Space (3.1 Launch through Uranus
Capture; 3.2 Arrival and Potential for Aerocapture; 3.3 Probe Delivery and Uranus System Tour); 4 Spacecraft Design Updates
(4.1 Reduced Power Generation; 4.2 Power Generation Alternatives; 4.3 Orbiter Mass Optimization; 4.4 Launch Vehicle and
Propulsion System Analysis; 4.5 Probe Design Maturation); 5 Updated Concept of Operations; 6 Conclusion and Next Steps;
Acknowledgments; Appendix (Tables A1 mass summary, A2 master equipment list, A3 orbital-operation power profiles);
References. Tables 1-5 in the body, Figures 1-12. Sections 4 and 5 and the appendix are about power, mass, probe design and
per-orbit operations, not trajectories.

## Trajectories inside the Uranus system (everything the paper says)

**Orbit insertion (Sec. 2.2, Fig. 2, Sec. 3.2).**
- "orbit insertion should be moved to a higher-altitude ring gap, completely outside of the rings, or away from the ring plane
  to reduce mission risk." (Sec. 2.2)
- Fig. 2 caption: "Example Uranus orbit insertion outside the zeta ring. Uranus orbit insertion maneuver traverses Uranus's
  ring plane in the epsilon-nu ring gap (left). Uranus orbit insertion (UOI) burn (1.62 hr duration, 1331 m s-1) as viewed from
  Earth (right)... Reproduced from D. Ellison et al. (2025)."
- "The large angles between the respective spacecraft and planet velocity vectors on arrival also place the periapse of the
  capture orbit, and at least a portion of the capture burn arc, on the night side of the planet" (Sec. 2.2).
- "For arrival dates near the upcoming equinox in early 2050, the spacecraft would be approaching Uranus roughly
  perpendicular to the ring/moon plane." (Sec. 2.2)
- Capture delta-v of the reference missions (Sec. 3.2): 2010 Decadal "1.66 km s-1", 2021 Decadal "1.09 km s-1"; aerocapture
  "begins to offer mass advantages when capture delta-v exceeds 2 km s-1"; conventional bipropellant "(<3 km s-1)". Aerocapture
  was rejected: "we concluded that aerocapture was too risky and not needed for our UOP design."
- Arrival V-infinity at Uranus in Table 1 (cruise options, not moon encounters): 6.3, 5, 6-8, 6-8 and 6 km/s.

**Probe delivery (Sec. 2.3, 3.3).**
- "the 2021 Decadal orbiter begins on a highly inclined orbit relative to the planet and delivers an atmospheric probe during
  the first orbit (after first apoapse)"; the post-capture periapse "must be raised prior to beginning the moon tour".
- Probe peak entry acceleration limited to 50 g (110 g in 2021); "reducing the entry vehicle's horizontal flight path angle
  compared to the -30.7 deg used in the 2021 Decadal reference design"; target "approximately -16 deg to -18 deg"; "The primary
  mechanism ... is to reduce the capture orbit latitude of periapse." Orbiter flyover passes "through the epsilon-nu ring gap
  during probe descent" at a higher altitude. Probe coast "~20-30 day" between orbiter separation and entry (Sec. 4.2).
  Detailed trajectories are deferred to Ellison et al. (2025) and Landau et al. (2025).

**Equatorialization / inclination reduction (Sec. 2.3).** The only passage:
"Repeated flybys of Titania were used to lower the orbital inclination and enable an in-plane satellite tour; other satellites
can be used for this purpose, though Titania, as the largest moon, is the most efficient (A. Simon et al. 2021). Near Uranus's
equinox, typical arrival geometries allow for accelerated equatorialization due to the low arrival asymptote declination
(decl.) relative to Uranus's equator during that time. This low decl. results in capture orbits that have a low periapse
latitude and that, therefore, require fewer satellite flybys to adjust the inclination for an equatorial system tour (D.
Ellison et al. 2025). As Uranus moves away from equinox, these lower decl. captures become increasingly difficult, and polar
tours become a more favorable option when considering overall flight time." No flyby count, inclination value, delta-v or
duration for equatorialization is given in this paper.

**Satellite tour (Sec. 2.3 and 3.3).** Described only in general terms. Quotes:
- "To meet science requirements, the 2021 Decadal study assumed a robust suite of orbiter instruments and a full tour of the
  Uranus system with flybys of the five largest moons." (Sec. 2.3)
- "recent satellite tour designs have focused on achieving the baseline requirements identified by the 2021 Decadal under a
  conservative set of operational assumptions. Example tour designs presented in D. Landau et al. (2025) and D. Ellison et al.
  (2025) demonstrate that a baseline tour is possible within 4 yr of probe delivery. Furthermore, it is possible to achieve the
  baseline tour requirements of three flybys per major satellite (Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon) in that time
  span, while maintaining a minimum of 30 days between encounters (D. Ellison et al. 2025), which accommodates the science data
  and telemetry downlink volume requirements ..." (Sec. 3.3)
- Reference orbit (Secs. 2.3, 4.1, 5, Abstract): "~34 day orbit" (2021 Decadal and Sec. 4.1 "~34 day reference orbit duration
  were retained"); Sec. 5 uses "a notional 33.3 day orbit"; Conclusion "each 33.3 day orbit". Sec. 5 describes three bounding
  per-orbit science cases: "(1) a satellite flyby-focused orbit, (2) an atmosphere and rings-focused orbit, and (3) a fields and
  particles-focused orbit", and says "Rather than attempting to optimize every orbit of a tour to meet the requirements of every
  objective, observations were instead interleaved to fill the orbit." Fig. 7 caption: "Concept of operations a satellite-focused
  orbit. Science observations were interleaved with downlinks, battery recharging, optical navigation (OpNav) images, and
  required orbital trim maneuvers (OTM) and reaction wheel desaturations (DSAT)". This is a power and data timeline for one
  orbit, not a moon sequence.
- Community poll cited (Sec. 5): "while a range of geometries is desired for each science objective, the close passes of the
  planet and major moons are a high priority (A. Simon et al. 2025)."

**Cruise material (outside the Uranus system, for completeness).** Table 1 (cruise options for 2035-2040 launch): Jupiter
assist E-(DV)EJ-U, 13 yr; Falcon Heavy direct E-U, 10-13 yr; inner-solar-system assist E-VEE-U, 13-14 yr; SEP E-E-U or E-EE-U,
12-14 yr; Starship direct E-U, ~10 yr. Direct Hohmann transfer "~16-17 yr". SEP stage separates after "(5 yr)".

## VERDICT-FACTS (facts only)

**(a) Occurrences of "cycler", "periodic", "free-return", "resonant", "repeat", "alternat".**
- "cycler": 0 occurrences. "free-return": 0. "resonan*": 0 (no "resonant" or "resonance" anywhere in the body).
- Referring to a trajectory inside the Uranus system: exactly 1 occurrence, Sec. 2.3: "Repeated flybys of Titania were used to
  lower the orbital inclination and enable an in-plane satellite tour". This is repeated flybys of a single moon (Titania) for
  inclination reduction in the 2021 Decadal design, not a periodic or two-moon geometry.
- Referring to something else (all other occurrences), counted as the number of words/phrases:
  - "periodic": 1, Sec. 2.3, "periodic interruptions required when the spacecraft was in downlink or propulsive maneuver mode"
    (instrument power).
  - "repeat*" about launch opportunities or windows: Abstract "most repeatable trajectory solutions"; Sec. 2 "highly repeatable
    launch opportunities"; Sec. 3.1 "do not repeat yearly with sufficient deliverable mass"; Table 1 row "Repeatability" with
    the entries "Only within narrow windows", "Repeatable any year" (three times), "Max mass not available yearly"; Fig. 3
    caption "repeatable launch windows". That is 5 prose occurrences plus the Table 1 row label and 3 table cells.
  - "alternat*": 22 occurrences in the body by a text search; those found in prose (Sec. 1 "alternative launch periods"; Sec. 2.1 "alternative mission architectures";
    Sec. 2.2 "alternative insertion location options"; Sec. 4 "architecture alternatives"; Sec. 4.2 "alternative power generation
    sources" and "Other alternatives" and "Am-RSG could offer a feasible alternative" and "alternative for the UOP orbiter";
    Sec. 4.3 "alternatives"; Sec. 4.4 "architectural alternatives", "component alternatives", "engine alternatives",
    "Other alternatives identified"; Sec. 4.5 "an alternate RF antenna"); all refer to mission architectures, power sources,
    launch periods or hardware, none to a trajectory in the Uranus system. (Some repeats are within the same sentences; none of
    the 22 refers to moons.)

**(b) Sentences naming two or more specific moons in a flyby sequence.**
- None that give a sequence. The only sentence naming several moons is the Sec. 3.3 sentence quoted above, which lists the five
  major satellites as a set ("three flybys per major satellite (Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, and Oberon)") with no order,
  no pairing and no repetition pattern. By text search of the body, Oberon, Umbriel, Ariel and Miranda each appear exactly once (in that
  list); Titania appears three times (that list and twice in the Sec. 2.3 inclination-reduction passage).

**(c) Does any table or figure give a moon-by-moon flyby list?** No table does (Tables 1-5 and A1-A3 are cruise options,
power scenarios, masses, probe masses, payload assumptions, equipment lists and power profiles). No figure caption (Figs. 1-12)
describes a flyby sequence: Fig. 1 approach angles, Fig. 2 orbit insertion, Fig. 3 SEP deliverable mass, Figs. 4-6 spacecraft
and probe configurations, Figs. 7-12 per-orbit power and data timelines. Figures were not opened.

## What it does NOT contain
- No flyby sequence, flyby dates, V-infinity at any moon, flyby altitudes or tour delta-v: the tour is delegated to Landau et al.
  (2025) and Ellison et al. (2025).
- No apo-twist, petal-rotation, period-reduction or inclination figures; no equatorialization duration or count.
- No cycler, periodic-orbit, free-return or resonant-return analysis for any Uranian moon or pair of moons; the words are absent
  (see VERDICT-FACTS).
- No orbit period other than the notional ~34 day (33.3 day) science orbit; no moon ephemeris or dynamical model; no search
  method description for tours.
- No Uranian-moon V-infinity values; the only V-infinity values are Uranus arrival values in Table 1 (5 to 8 km/s).
- Cruise trajectories are summarised in Table 1 with details deferred to Englander et al. (2026, printed "JSpRo, submitted").
