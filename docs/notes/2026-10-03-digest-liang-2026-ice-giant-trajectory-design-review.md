## What it is
A literature review. Abstract (p.1): "This survey systematically synthesizes both established and state-of-the-art methods across four primary mission phases: interplanetary transfer, ice giant capture, planetary satellite tours, and other scientific observations targeting the planets and comets." Its stated claim of priority (p.6): "to the best of the author's knowledge, a systematic compilation and comprehensive review of trajectory design for ice giant system exploration missions remains absent from the literature." It is a methods and mission-concept review at the level of summary paragraphs. No new computation. Only about 5 of its 44 pages cover in-system tours and moon-based trajectories (Section 4, pp.20-25, plus parts of 3.1 and 5.2).

## (2) Satellite tours and in-system trajectories at Uranus and Neptune — every study reviewed
Reference numbers are the manuscript's. The manuscript has several citation-number mismatches with its own reference list (see the end of this section); where the text and the list disagree it is noted.

### Uranus

**McAdams et al. [30], 2011, "Conceptual mission design of a polar Uranus orbiter and satellite tour" (Spaceflight Mechanics).** What was designed: a Uranus orbiter tour from 2033 (p.20). Model: "The tour trajectory is designed using a patched-conic model with satellite ephemeris locations". Numbers quoted (p.20-21): Uranus orbital insertion (UOI) burned "to insert the spacecraft into a 20.5-day orbit around Uranus with the periapsis of 1.3 RU and the inclination of 97.7 deg"; "a 424-day baseline satellite tour with 10 targeted flybys, including Ariel, Umbriel, Titania, Oberon, and Miranda"; "the total Delta-v is about 619 m/s". Resonances: "the spacecraft will fly the same satellite repeatedly within resonant orbits (16:1 for Miranda, 10:1 for Ariel, 6:1 for Umbriel, 3:1 for Titania, and 2:1 for Oberon) and transfer to another by maneuvers"; "The gravity assist is mainly used to change the shape of the orbits rather than the inclination." Turning: "For the biggest two satellites, Oberon and Titania, only ~1 deg of turning for a hyperbolic flyby is allowed, while for others, the turn angle will be ~0.25 deg or less [30]. Therefore, a Delta-v of 80~180 m/s is necessary for each transfer between two different satellites." An extra 5 m/s "is allocated for each flyby" for navigation errors. The review's criticisms: "the satellite tour design is not optimized end-to-end" (so the minimum-Delta-v solution "may not have been discovered"); the high inclination leaves "the only encounter location for the spacecraft and the satellite to be the intersection of the two orbits", with "very high excess velocity" and a "narrow encounter window"; high-fidelity real-trajectory and navigation error "is not studied deeply". (The review's sentence here says "due to the inclination of Neptune's axis of rotation", which is as printed, in the Uranus section.) Also in 5.1 (p.26): used "the first 20 orbits after the Uranus insertion to determine the parameters of the gravity field", then changed periapsis "to improve the determination of the Uranus gravity field J6 term"; B-plane targeting [30].

**Turrini et al. [71], 2014, "The comparative exploration of the ice giant planets with twin spacecraft" (Planetary and Space Science 104:93-107).** Conception only: "the detailed trajectory design has not been elaborated" (p.21). Chemical propulsion plus gravity assist; "the nominal satellite tour will last three years"; moderate-eccentricity capture: "the first is to take 1.5-2 years to investigate the satellites, and the second is to take 1-1.5 years to focus on the exploration of the planets and their ring systems"; high eccentricity: planets and rings near periapsis, satellites farther out. The NASA study tour cited as [28] "is 2 years long" and "could allow for two flybys of each of five major satellites of the ice giant planet". Model: not stated.

**McCarty et al. [32], 2018, kilopower electric propulsion mission design (AAS/AIAA Astrodynamics Specialist Conference).** In-system part (p.21): "a brief analysis of reaching a circular orbit around Titania rather than a tour"; "a 3000 kg spacecraft could take about 400 days to achieve a 600 km circular orbit around Titania with kilopower electric propulsion using 270 kg of propellant." Model: not stated. (Its interplanetary part, 2025-2045 windows, Lambert grid search with 2-4 flybys, arrival excess velocity set to zero, is on p.9.)

**Landau et al. [29], 2025, "Uranus Cruise and Tour Design Impacts on Science, Cost, and Risk" (IEEE Aerospace Conference).** What was designed: moon tours and magneto-tours (pp.21-22). Model: "the patched-conics algorithm is used to develop a large solution space of tours." Guidelines: minimum flyby altitude "of 50 km"; check "whether the occurrence of the flyby is on an inbound or outbound node". Numbers: "The whole tour starts on 6 Sep 2045 at the Titania flyby and lasts for about 700 days, comprising 3 flybys of Miranda, 7 of Ariel, 4 of Umbriel, 8 of Titania, and 4 of Oberon. Most flybys of the same satellite are resonant. The moon tour not only provides the opportunities to observe the satellites at a close distance but also reduces the orbital inclination to near zero degrees." Magneto-tour: "to use Titania gravity assists to rotate the petal (apoapsis)". Broad-search trade results: "The moon tours require 100~800 m/s and 1~4 years. While the magneto-tours are considered, some moon tours are not available. The total Delta-v ranges from 200 m/s to 800 m/s, and the duration is 2.5~5 years." Also a "Uranus close pass phase" (p.26) and probe release at "the first revolution after insertion" (p.26). Note: in Section 2.1 (p.8) the manuscript attributes the Venus/Earth/Mars-flyby interplanetary scenario to "Damon et al. [29]", while reference [29] is Landau et al.; see mismatches.

**UOP study [1] (Simon, Nimmo, Anderson et al., 2021 Final Report), via 3.1 (p.16).** "The noncoplanar capture case firstly appears in the UOP mission concept [1]. The main operation is still an initial impulse capture maneuver, but the Titania's gravity assists are leveraged in about two years to achieve plane alignment."

**Zhang, Li & Baoyin [54], 2025, "Noncoplanar Capture Trajectory Optimization in Uranian System Exploration Mission" (IEEE Trans. Aerosp. Electron. Syst.).** Capture using Titania (p.16): "an optimized three-impulse and four-impulse transfer strategy for capture. The impulse and resonant Titania gravity assists are used to reduce the inclination from 69.9 deg to 0 deg in one year, where each Titania flyby could reduce about 2-5 deg from 33.4 deg to 0 deg. The v-infinity of the Titania flyby is about 4.885 km/s, while the maximum velocity change provided by gravity assist is 109.5 m/s. The effect of other Uranus satellites' gravity assist will be weaker." Model: not stated. Review's judgement: "the satellite-aided capture is gloomy to be applied, but it also has the potential to reduce the orbital inclination to aim at the equatorial plane of Uranus." Also 3.1 (p.16): "there is usually a large angle difference between the satellite orbit plane and the spacecraft's entry trajectory plane. The flyby can only occur at the intersection of two planes. That means the multi-satellite-aided capture is not available, and the energy reduction would be little due to the large excess velocity" and "there is little research on leveraging satellite-aided capture for Uranus system exploration."

Other Uranus statements: "Titania ... even less than one-tenth of that of the smallest Galilean moon, Europa" (p.15-16); Titania and Oberon "diameters are both larger than 1500 km" (p.20); inclination trade-off (p.22): "high-inclination orbit limits the design space of satellite tours. This makes multiple flybys of different satellites difficult." Conclusions (p.29-30): "The Uranus system presents a wider array of tour design options than Neptune. Mission architects can design Uranian tours for multiple satellites or dedicated single-moon science".

### Neptune

**Rymer et al. Neptune Odyssey [15] (and [11]), 2020/2021.** Tour (p.23): "consists of 46 Triton flybys to provide near-global coverage of Triton [15]. The tour is divided into 5 phases", described as shortening the orbital period, "resonant flying by to cover the sub-Neptune hemisphere, transferring to the opposite side of Neptune, resonant flying by to cover the anti-Neptune hemisphere, and reducing the orbital period to approach the rings", ending in disposal "by Neptune atmospheric entry". "The required Delta-v of the whole tour is about 50.136 m/s." Model: not stated. Interplanetary part of the same concept (p.13-14): direct Earth-to-Neptune transfer, "8.6 km/s maneuver", "245 m/s" inclination maneuver, 15.9-year cruise, "excess velocity of about 6.5 km/s".

**Campagnola et al. [38], 2015, "Mission design for the exploration of Neptune and Triton" (IEEE AES Magazine 30(7):6-17).** Tour (pp.23-24): "an example moon tour for 2 years, which only includes Triton flybys", "designed using patched-conic approximation and Triton ephemerides and divided into three phases." Phase 1: crank-over-the-top (COT) flybys "at almost the same Triton orbital location" to change inclination "from 160 deg to 115 deg and then back to 160 deg"; "The 20 Triton flybys in the first phase concentrated on the sunlit Neptune-facing hemisphere with the altitudes between ~150 and ~1000 km and the pericenter radius of 7,500~250,000 km. The excess speed is chosen as ~3 km [sic, units as printed] to increase the maximum of available inclination with the COT [75]." Phase 2: "14 planar flybys ... according to the 'petal strategy', which occurs along Triton's orbit at intervals of ~30 deg"; ends with a flyby on the opposite side of Triton's orbit. Phase 3: "21 COT flybys" back from 160 to 115 deg and reset to 160 deg for the sunlit anti-Neptune-facing hemisphere. Delta-v: "Although the entire tour can be completed without the requirement for maneuvering theoretically", about 250 m/s is allocated for navigation (each flyby plus large Triton ephemeris uncertainty), and "an extra Delta-v of ~300 m/s is required" when the Triton orbit phase is added. (The text names the authors "Stefano et al." here.) Review's criticism: "the design guidelines of the 2 COT phases are not introduced in detail, and the coverage assessment of the observation of Triton is insufficient." Also: "Flyby detection around a single satellite is also a fuel-saving and repetitive detection method, which is mainly used in the exploration of Triton [88][38]" (p.28). Reference [38] is also used for the Neptune interplanetary window analysis (p.13).

**Miceli, Bosanac, Stuart et al. [76], 2024, "Motion Primitive Approach to Spacecraft Trajectory Design in the Neptune-Triton System" (AIAA SciTech 2024, 1280).** The only dynamical-systems study at an ice-giant moon reviewed. Model (p.24): "the Neptune-Triton circular restricted three-body problem is used as the dynamical model". Approach (p.24-25): "Compute 'motion primitives', which are the natural arcs along the families of the periodical orbits and their invariant manifolds, and store them in a library. 2) Based on the technology of graph theory, construct a graph of motion primitives and evaluate their connectivity. 3) Search the motion primitive graph and select the motion primitive sequences to scrabble up an initial guess of the trajectory. 4) Correct the trajectory through maneuver to connect the endpoint and starting point of the trajectory segments." Initial state: adjusted "to be in the Neptune-Triton plane and avoid collision with Neptune, rather than adopting the original initial state with an inclination of 20.64 deg relative to the Neptune-Triton plane." Target: "to perform magnetic induction measurement ... the target orbit is selected to be a planar 3:4 resonant orbit", chosen for "the minimum Jacobi constant and a similar direction of motion relative to the initial arrival in the rotating system". Result: "three transfers with the minimum Delta-v are provided by the authors. The transfer time and the total maneuver are demonstrated to be as low as 12.6 days and 1.64 km/s, respectively." Priors it mentions: the approach "has already been used in the Earth-Moon system to design the trajectory like the optimal transfers between the periodical orbits [77]" (Smith and Bosanac 2023). Review's assessment: "Miceli et al. designed the trajectory leveraging more of the dynamic characteristics of the system itself and applied the motion primitive approach in a new scene. However, the author's work focuses more on the application of the transfer trajectories design method. Therefore, the tour design is relatively simple. Possible future works include expanding complex tour designs and studying the application of the method in a higher-fidelity model of the Neptune-Triton system."

**Melman et al. [37], 2007, "Trajectory optimization for a mission to Neptune and Triton" (AIAA/AAS Astrodynamics Specialist Conference, 7366).** Triton capture in the CR3BP (p.28): "a method called energy surface reconstruction to design a low-energy capture trajectory to Triton in the Circular Restricted Three Body Problem (CR3BP), and the stability and instability property near the libration points is the key to the trajectory design." Target: "a nearly circular orbit around Triton with a semi-major axis of 1900 km and an inclination of 70 deg". "The whole transfer from an elliptical orbit around Neptune includes six maneuvers, and the total Delta-v is 2850 m/s. The detection mission is designed to last for six months, and the required maintenance Delta-v is 31 m/s, which increases with the inclination." Also (p.16): "when the spacecraft arrives at Neptune with the orbital energy of about 75 km2/s2, the achievable change utilized by Triton is only 0.7515 km2/s2 [37]." Also quoted: Yang et al. [4] expected speed reduction "leveraging the gravitational perturbation of multiple celestial bodies is 10% compared to the traditional capture method" (p.16). The text also cites [37] for small-satellite flybys "interspersed in the middle of the tour design" (p.28).

**Bienstock et al. [90], 2008, "Neptune orbiter, probe, and Triton Lander mission".** (p.28) "the total required Delta-v to capture into a 200 km circular Triton orbit after aerocapture is about 2106.2 km/s [sic, as printed], where 1680 m/s of Delta-v is used in the powered Triton flyby and 407 m/s of Delta-v is used to capture into the orbit around Triton." Triton lander evaluated.

**Oleson and Landis [8], 2018, Triton Hopper.** (pp.28-29, 13) "The lander is deorbited from a 200 km circular orbit. The Hopper could hop about 30 times for a distance of 150 km, which means one lander could sample and evaluate 30 locations in a two-year mission." Interplanetary: "About 2610 kg of Xe propellant is required for the 12-year interplanetary cruise." Review notes Neptune Odyssey "excluded the Triton lander".

**China concept [18] (Yang M, Wang C, Gao F et al.), p.5.** "After reaching Neptune, the spacecraft utilized Triton's gravity assist to reduce the capture velocity increment, entering an elliptical orbit around Neptune with an inclination of approximately 70 degrees ... By repeatedly leveraging resonant flyby to reduce the apoapsis altitude, global observations of Neptune and Triton are conducted. The probe eventually entered a 2:1 resonance orbit around Triton, making long-term remote sensing observations of Neptune and Triton." Direct transfer launch around 2033 with "C3 ... higher than 158 km2/s2" reaching Neptune "within 15 years". Model: not stated.

Other Neptune statements (p.23): Triton is "the only big enough satellite"; Proteus "mass is only about 0.2% of that of Triton"; Nereid eccentricity "0.7512"; "the gravity-assist satellite tour necessitated a design centered on Triton. Other satellite flybys will change little velocity". Conclusions (p.30): "a Neptunian tour is predominantly dedicated to achieving global mapping of Triton."

**Future-work passage, quoted in full with its surrounding sentences (Section 6, "Other scientific trajectories", pp.31-32):**
"(3) Other scientific trajectories. The current repertoire of trajectories for ice giant system exploration is limited in variety and fails to satisfy the scientific curiosity about these distant planets. Moreover, existing research has not conducted in-depth investigations into the orbital dynamics characteristics within ice giant systems, and studies on the long-term evolutionary properties of orbits remain insufficient. **Passage on multi-body methods in other systems (future work (1), pp.30-31):** "It is nearly impossible to change orbits near the ice giant using solar energy. Therefore, designing low-velocity increment trajectories is a challenge that must be addressed for ice giant exploration. In current research, gravity-assist technology is the most predominantly employed key technique in ice giant exploration missions. However, this utilization of natural celestial forces is typically analyzed within the two-body problem framework. Incorporating the gravitational forces of multiple bodies--such as massive moons and the Sun--can facilitate the design of more fuel-efficient trajectories, which has been widely studied in other systems [91][92][93][94][95][96][97]. However, the motion of spacecraft with more complex and nonlinear dynamics becomes increasingly chaotic. ## (6) What the review says is missing or future work for ice-giant in-system trajectory design (quotes)
- Conclusions, satellite tour phase (p.30): "However, the problem of optimizing a mission across multiple, often conflicting objectives like timeline and observational value, while searching an immense solution space, poses a significant challenge."
- Conclusions, capture phase (p.30): "In ice giant systems, due to the low mass of their natural satellites, satellite-aided capture cannot provide significant deceleration. It is, therefore, primarily employed for orbital inclination adjustment following the primary insertion maneuver."
- Future (1), multi-natural forces (pp.30-31): the passage quoted in Section (4) above, ending "Additionally, alternative propulsion methods utilizing magnetic forces, such as tether propulsion, can also achieve propellant savings but have not yet been extensively studied in the scene of ice giants' exploration. Consequently, the integrated use of multiple natural forces in complex mission design requires further investigation."
- Future (2), robustness (p.31): "However, there are few studies on the robust trajectory optimization method for ice giant exploration currently. The consideration of uncertainties typically remains confined to the allocation of additional propellant budget rather than motivating deeper investigation."
- Future (3), other scientific trajectories (pp.31-32): quoted in full in Section (3).
- On the Uranus tour work specifically (p.20-21, McAdams): not optimised end-to-end; high-inclination geometry restricts encounters; real-trajectory and navigation errors not deeply studied. On Neptune (p.24, Campagnola): COT-phase design guidelines not detailed, coverage assessment insufficient. On Miceli (p.25): "the tour design is relatively simple. Possible future works include expanding complex tour designs and studying the application of the method in a higher-fidelity model of the Neptune-Triton system."
- On the apse-line effect of close passes at an oblate planet (p.26-27): "There is currently rare research on the impact of arch line rotation."
- The Triton lander (p.29): "how to deal with the potential risk and enhance the scientific value of detection is a key issue to be researched in the future."
